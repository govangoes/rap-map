How this reads rhymes

Every word is broken into syllables and each syllable’s vowel sound is looked up in the CMU Pronouncing Dictionary, the public-domain pronunciation list used in speech research. Slang isn’t in a standard dictionary, so common rap and spoken-English words (finna, bruh, goated) come from a hand-written list, and endings like -in’ and -a for -er follow dialect rules. That list is still short and growing. Anything else that isn’t covered falls back to a spelling-based guess, so check those by ear.

Multisyllabic mode slides a window of 2–4 syllables across each line and colors any run of vowel sounds that repeats elsewhere in the verse, even across word boundaries (say yes / play test). Three tiers: italic means only the broad vowel family matched (a slant rhyme), plain color means the exact vowels matched, and bold means the ending consonants matched too, the tightest rhyme. End rhyme mode only looks at the last stressed syllable of each line and labels them A/B/C like a rhyme scheme.

Paste your own lyrics to see how many different words you use. Add a second set to compare two songs, two artists, or old you versus new you.

How these numbers work

Words are lowercased and counted as written, so run and running count as different words. Unique-word share is unique words divided by total words; it drops as lyrics get longer, so use unique words in the first 1,000 to compare sets of different lengths (it needs at least 1,000 words). One-offs are words used exactly once. The most-used list skips common filler like “the” and “and”.
